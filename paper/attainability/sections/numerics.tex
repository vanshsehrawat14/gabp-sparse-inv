\section{Exactness is not accuracy}
\label{sec:numerics}

The finite precision grid below shows no consistent advantage in either
direction.
It is a diagnostic, rather than a universal comparison of sparse and dense
factorizations.

Scored on the pattern against a 64-bit oracle and swept over conditioning
(Table~\ref{tab:precision}), the selected inverse and a dense SPD solve at
equal 32-bit floating-point (fp32) precision stay within small constant
factors of each other across
structures and $\kappa$: the error ratio ranges from $0.34$ to $1.95$ with no
consistent direction. At the highest-$\kappa$ grid cell the dense solve is in fact
the more accurate, by more than $2.8\times$. An initial reading on ill-scaled random matrices suggested roughly
$1.9\times$ on trees, growing with $\kappa$. Over enough seeds (16 for the
ordering study) that reading turned out to be a ratio-of-medians artifact. Most
of what remains, a modest and heavy-tailed median gap, is an
elimination-ordering effect: a dense Cholesky run in the kernel's own
leaf-first order has comparable accuracy. No consistent accuracy edge appears
on this grid. The 16-seed ordering diagnostic is recorded in
\href{https://github.com/vanshsehrawat14/gabp-sparse-inv/blob/main/paper/attainability/figures/data/precision_compare_orders.txt}
{\nolinkurl{paper/attainability/figures/data/precision_compare_orders.txt}}.

The relevant distinction is structural. The operator is algebraically exact,
global, and sparse-structured, which makes it usable as a layer:
Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} shows forward timings consistent with the chain's
$\bigO(n)$ operation count, with the measured dense crossover, and
forward-plus-backward Gaussian Markov random field (GMRF) training timings
consistent with linear tree work
where dense autograd is $\bigO(N^3)$. The cost still grows with problem size,
linearly on the chain shown here; it does not acquire an iteration count
proportional to graph distance.
